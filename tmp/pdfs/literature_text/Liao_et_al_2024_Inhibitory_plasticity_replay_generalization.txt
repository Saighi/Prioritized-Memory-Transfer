Inhibitory plasticity supports replay 
generalization in the hippocampus
Zhenrui Liao    1,2,3,4,8 , Satoshi Terada1,2,8, Ivan Georgiev Raikov    5,6,8, 
Darian Hadjiabadi    5,6,8, Miklos Szoboszlay    1,2, Ivan Soltesz    6,7  & 
Attila Losonczy    1,2 
Memory consolidation assimilates recent experiences into long-term 
memory. This process requires the replay of learned sequences, although 
the content of these sequences remains controversial. Recent work has 
shown that the statistics of replay deviate from those of experience: stimuli 
that are experientially salient may be either recruited or suppressed 
from sharp-wave ripples. In this study, we found that this phenomenon 
can be explained parsimoniously and biologically plausibly by a Hebbian 
spike-time-dependent plasticity rule at inhibitory synapses. Using models at 
three levels of abstraction—leaky integrate-and-fire, biophysically detailed 
and abstract binary—we show that this rule enables efficient generalization, 
and we make specific predictions about the consequences of intact and 
perturbed inhibitory dynamics for network dynamics and cognition. 
Finally, we use optogenetics to artificially implant non-generalizable 
representations into the network in awake behaving mice, and we find that 
these representations also accumulate inhibition during sharp-wave ripples, 
experimentally validating a major prediction of our model. Our work 
outlines a potential direct link between the synaptic and cognitive levels 
of memory consolidation, with implications for both normal learning and 
neurological disease.
Humans and other animals have the remarkable ability to learn about 
the world from observations containing a mix of structural features of 
the environment and distractions that do not generalize. The ability  
to learn arbitrary sequences and relational maps, and to deploy them 
to guide behavior, is the defining computational function of the hippo-
campus1– 5. A subset of hippocampal neurons, termed ‘place cells’ , 
exhibit selective tuning to discrete locations in space: as an animal 
explores its environment, the sequence of place cell firing reflects 
the animalʼ s path 6. The hippocampus later reinstates previously 
learned sequences in temporally compressed ‘replay’ epochs inside 
high-frequency oscillatory events known as sharp-wave ripples (SWRs)7 
during both sleep8–11  and awake rest12–15 . This replay is required for the 
long-term consolidation of memories, although how memories are 
selected for consolidation has been the subject of much debate16–19 .
The strongly recurrently connected20– 23 CA3 and CA2 regions of 
the hippocampus are thought to be the source of SWRs and the drivers 
of replay throughout the hippocampus7,24– 26. Despite this, replay has 
mostly been investigated within the hippocampal CA1 circuitry 7– 10,13 
with fewer studies reporting replay in CA3/CA2 (refs. 14 ,25,27,28), 
largely due to practical constraints. Recurrent excitatory weight 
updates in CA3 occur by a symmetric spike-timing-dependent plasticity 
(sSTDP) rule29, providing a plausible explanation for the mix of forward

neurons in our network may be tuned to place, cue, both or neither, as

to the distance between their tuning peaks. Meanwhile, the distractor 
cell subnetwork becomes strongly recurrently coupled under the same

T o simulate the offline state (Fig. 1f, green highlight and inset), PCs 
were driven with random Poisson spiking input irrespective of their 
distractor, place or untuned status, simulating unstructured input from 
mossy fibers (MFs) (Fig. 1c). We found that place sequences encoun-
tered during learning are spontaneously replayed in the forward and 
backward directions offline in our network (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, we 
found that the distractor-tuned neurons, despite being generally active 
in the offline period, are selectively suppressed during spontaneous 
ripples (Fig. 1g). This is true of both exclusive distractor cells and cells 
tuned to both distractors and place, consistent with experimental 
findings (Supplementary Fig. 1). Spontaneous replay in the network 
was robust to variations in the exact I → E plasticity rule used, so long 
as the plasticity rule was a Hebbian L TP rule operating on a timescale 
of roughly 3 ms (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).
In summary, we show that replay spontaneously emerges in a 
randomly recurrently connected network trained on a simulated ran-
dom cue-spatial (RC-S) task using sSTDP of excitatory and inhibitory 
synapses. We further show that, even though this replay is completely 
spontaneous and internal to the network, with no explicit externally 
applied ‘replay signalʼ, and the learning rule is strictly local to individual 
synapses, the network self-organizes distributed and specific suppres-
sion of cue cells during replay epochs.
Biophysically detailed model of CA3 replay
We next asked whether sSTDP of recurrent E → E and I → E synapses 
is sufficient to suppress distractor inputs during offline replay in a 
biophysical model of the hippocampal CA3 network. We modeled CA3 
using 260 multi-comparmental and biophysically detailed pyramidal 
neurons62 and 30 INs with biophysically realistic electrophysiological 
properties63,64 (Methods). During online learning, PCs and INs received 
input structured into distinct dendritic input streams, consistent with 
in vivo data64– 68. Due to the higher computational cost of simulating 
three-dimensional (3D) biophysically detailed cells, we overrepre -
sented the populations of interest (place and cue cells) to make our 
simulations more tractable. Specifically, 80% of PCs received spatially 
structured place and grid input via an MF and medial entorhinal cortical 
(MEC) pathway, respectively69– 71. The remaining 20% of PCs received a 
distractor input via a lateral entorhinal cortical (LEC) pathway71 (Fig. 2a). 
As in Fig. 1, the PCs receiving distractor input (that is, cue cells) were 
driven at a randomly selected location during each lap of the linear 
virtual track. After online learning with the sSTDP rule, place cell firing 
increased on average to physiologically relevant firing rates (0.264 Hz 
to 1.087 Hz) and, furthermore, robustly tiled the track (Fig. 2b) with 
little out-of-field firing (Fig. 2c).
T o investigate the offline dynamics of cue cells and place cells, 
we simulated a single ‘online’ lap during which the network received 
structured input followed by an ‘offline’ state in which the network 
received only unstructured random inputs (Fig. 2a). The ‘online’ lap 
confirmed that the network retained place cell and cue cell selectivity  
obtained during training. During the offline state, epochs of high 
place cell activity spontaneously emerged (Fig. 2e). Sequences of 
and backward (temporally reversed) replay sequences observed experi-
mentally13,30. At the same time, modeling work has demonstrated the 
feasibility of obtaining spontaneous replay in biologically realistic 
spiking network models31–33 .
Inhibition is also known to play a major role in SWR generation34,35, 
although comparatively little is known about inhibitory dynamics in 
CA3/CA2. Under the standard model of learning in neural circuits, 
Hebbian plasticity remodels excitatory–excitatory synapses against 
a relatively static inhibitory background36,37. This model does not tra-
ditionally include plasticity at the GABAergic synapse, as a constant 
inhibitory background is presumed to be necessary for learning; how-
ever, this type of plasticity has been reported in vitro38,39, and a growing 
body of experimental and theoretical work has suggested a role for 
inhibitory plasticity in shaping network-level computations40–48 .
As a densely connected recurrent network with writeable weights, 
CA3/CA2 is a natural system in which to study the emergence, content 
and function of replay from a computational perspective. In the present 
study, we consider different perspectives on replay, including replay of 
predictive representations and specific past experiences49– 54, through 
the lens of generalization. We argue that, in a world where no two expe-
riences are exactly alike, replay consolidates the features and structure 
of the environment that are preserved across specific experiences, in 
essence sampling from the animalʼs working model of its world. This 
conceptual model synthesizes elements of both of these theories with 
prior work on model-based replay to explain how replay can be useful 
for guiding future behavior, even though representations from distal 
past experience that are not imminently behaviorally relevant are 
sometimes replayed 54– 56. In support of this conceptual framework, 
we present three levels of modeling—spiking network, biophysical 
and abstract normative—reproducing existing experimental find -
ings and demonstrating consolidation of generalizable structure in 
recurrently connected models of CA3. Central to all three approaches 
is our major prediction: inhibitory synapses are also remodeled by 
the spike-time-dependent long-term potentiation (L TP) rule, which 
enables the statistical structure of the world to be learned dynami -
cally from experience. We then tested this hypothesis using a novel 
sparse optogenetic cue cell induction approach and found that cells 
artificially entrained to statistically unpredictable stimuli accumulate 
inhibition in SWRs. Taken together, our results in this work posit a role 
for the L TP of inhibitory synapses in the hippocampus in a high-level 
cognitive function.
Results
Network model of CA3 replay
We first built a large, randomly recurrently connected CA3 spiking 
network capable of internally generating spontaneous replay32,33. We 
simulated an experiment in which the CA3 network was exposed to 
both spatial input and random distractor stimuli in an online phase 
during which synaptic weights were learned by sSTDP. Then, in a sub-
sequent offline phase, learned patterns were read out from spontane-
ous replay (Fig. 1a and ref. 57). Our network consisted of 8,000 leaky 
integrate-and-fire (LIF) pyramidal cells (PCs) and 150 interneurons 
(INs) in Brian 2 (ref. 58) with spike-rate adaptation32,33,59, with 50% of the 
cells assigned to receive place input and a (non-mutually exclusive) 10% 
receiving stochastic distractor cue input57 (Fig. 1b). These neurons were 
randomly recurrently connected, with a connection probability of 10% 
between PCs as well as from PCs onto INs and a connection probability 
of 25% between INs as well as from INs onto PCs (Methods).
The simulated experiment consisted of 40 laps through a 3-m-long 
environment at constant velocity. Untuned cells fired at a baseline rate 
of 0.1 Hz (sampled from a homogeneous Poisson process) throughout 
the belt. The spike trains of place cells were sampled from an inhomo-
geneous Poisson process, with a position-dependent Poisson rate λ(x) 
(maximum 20 Hz) chosen as a circular Gaussian representing the cellʼs 
tuning curve. The spike trains of distractor-tuned cells were sampled in

Perturbations and predictions from the biophysical network 
model
Our central synaptic-level prediction is the existence of a Hebbian L TP 
rule of GABAergic synapses in the hippocampus that is required for 
the network-level phenomenon of replay and memory consolidation 
from noisy observations. Here, we performed a series of perturbation 
simulations to test the hypothesis that inhibitory plasticity is necessary 
for cue cell suppression and, more generally, world structure learning. 
Furthermore, the simulations performed here broaden the main find-
ings and generate additional experimentally verifiable predictions that 
give insight on neural communication across multiple spatial scales.

cells was 38.9% greater than onto place cells (median wI→ cue: 0.0063 uS; 
median wI→ place: 0.0046 uS; P = 2.5 × 10−187 , one-sided Mann–Whitney 
U-test) (Fig. 3a). Therefore, we performed simulated dual patch-clamp

enables cue cells to be suppressed during SWRs. PCs were held in 
voltage-clamp mode with holding voltages ranging from −65 mV to 
−45 mV in +5-mV increments. We observed that IPSCs onto cue cells 
(n = 960 measured pairs) were significantly greater (one-sided Mann–

Given the observation of powerful feedforward inhibition onto 
cue cells, we next predicted that targeted ablation of inhibitory plas-
ticity will impair the network mechanism of adaptive stimulus 57. In 
agreement with the prediction and further supporting the over -
arching hypothesis that inhibitory plasticity is necessary for world 
structure learning, models trained with only recurrent E → E plasticity 
exhibited correlated activity across the cue cell and place cell popula-
tions during the offline epoch (Fig. 3d–f; Pearson’s r = 0.1, P = 0.71). As 
a control, networks trained with E  → E plasticity only but containing 
few cue cells (<3% of total PC population) were still able to generate 
spontaneous replay events in the forward and reverse directions 
(Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig 5, top row), similar 
to ref. 32. Additional simulations reported that restoring inhibitory 
plasticity restores the adaptive stimulus mechanism (Supplementary 
Fig. 5, bottom row).
T o continue testing alternative mechanisms of cue cell suppres-
sion, we performed a simulation of the model with E  → E  and E  → I 
plasticity (Fig. 3g–i). Similar to the model with only E  → E plasticity 
(Fig. 3d–f ), cue cells and place cells were significantly co-active dur -
ing SWRs (r = 0.75, P = 6.2 × 10−234 ). The collective results of Fig. 3d–i  
and Supplementary Fig. 5 support the hypothesis that inhibitory 
plasticity is necessary for cue cell suppression. Additionally, from 
this simulation experiment (Fig. 3g–i), we predict that IN firing rates 
should stay stable during learning, which can be experimentally 
verified through a multitude of electrophysiological and/or optical 
methods.

T o summarize, we showed that inhibitory plasticity is both suffi-
cient (Fig. 2) and necessary (Fig. 3d–i) for cue cell suppression during 
SWRs. Additionally, we provide numerous predictions that can be 
tested experimentally to gather evidence that such a mechanism exists 
and plays a critical role in memory consolidation. At the cellular level, 
inhibitory plasticity from I → E synapses should lead to powerful feed-
forward inhibition onto cue cells, manifesting in the form of increased 
IPSCs onto cue cells compared to place cells (Fig. 3a–c). Furthermore, 
because models trained with E → E and E → I plasticity did not result in 
cue cell suppression, IN firing rates should stay stable during learning 
(Fig. 3g–i ). At the network level, ablating inhibitory plasticity (Fig. 3d–f) 
should result in loss of cue cell suppression that reemerges by restoring 
inhibitory plasticity (Supplementary Fig. 5). At the behavioral level, this 
suggests that ablating inhibitory plasticity would hinder an animal’s 
ability to perform world structure inference, leading to impairment 
for goal-oriented behavioral tasks. Taken together, these simulations 
highlight how synaptic-level mechanisms can cascade to affect network 
function across multiple spatial scales.
Simplified model and theoretical analysis
We next sought to provide intuitions about how our model generates 
spontaneous replay and the role that inhibitory plasticity plays in struc-
tural learning in the presence of noise.
Consider a maximally abstracted world consisting of a set of  
‘schemas’ (that may correspond to physical space, conjunctive context, 
sensory cues or abstract concepts). For simplicity, suppose that there 
is a one-to-one correspondence between schemas in the outside world 
and tuned ‘concept neurons’ in the brain, which we represent with 
colored circles in Fig. 4a. Also suppose that a ‘synapse’ exists between 
any pair of distinct neurons (Fig. 4a, weight matrix). The generalizable 
structure to be learned is simply some sequence of schemas.
T o learn this structure, the network modifies the synaptic weight 
matrix, W, in response to observations drawn from a generative process 
parameterized by the ground truth sequence.

In an ideal world, observations consist only of repeated sequential 
presentations of the ground truth sequence. In this case, a Hebbian L TP 
rule on only excitatory synapses is sufficient to learn this sequence: 
every time a transition a → b is observed, weights Wab and Wba are both 
incremented by 1. This process potentiates weights along the diagonal 
between cells representing adjacent elements of the sequence (Fig. 4a, 
right; compare to Fig. 1e, left). In the offline state, the network receives 
random input, which may by chance excite an arbitrary neuron x 1 in 
the network. Without loss of generality, we let x 1 be a one-hot vector 
(a vector of length N with 1 in the position corresponding to the active 
neuron and 0 everywhere else) at the first schema in the sequence. 
We then run the network for K  timesteps (where K  is the number of 
schemas in the sequence), letting the network follow its internal 
dynamics xt = σ(Wxt−1 ), where σ  is a stochastic nonlinearity that returns 
a one-hot vector whose non-zero element represents the next active 
neuron, with probabilities proportional to the x t−1 -th column of W 
(Methods). Adaptation in the models of Figs. 1 and 2 gives sequences 
‘momentum’ by preventing network reactivations from sticking in 
place or doubling back; to model adaptation abstractly, for each event 
we use only either the lower half-diagonal of W, corresponding to the 
forward weight matrix W FWD, or the upper diagonal, corresponding 
to the reverse weight matrix W REV. The output sequence is an offline 
statistical estimator of the ground truth sequence, which we term

A more realistic observation model contains noise: distractor 
observations may appear stochastically at different locations in the 
sequence. Using the previous Hebbian L TP rule, when distractor sche-
mas are observed, off-diagonal weights are potentiated in the weight 
matrix (Fig. 4b , right; compare to Fig. 1e, right); these off-diagonal 
weights create artificial ‘side paths’ through the network that do not 
follow the world structure path. Because the stochastic distractor can 
appear before or after any other schema, it eventually creates poten-
tial paths between any two observed schemas in the network as well 
as cycles at replay time. Because REPLAY samples the weight matrix, 
aberrant paths in the network translate into replay of disorganized, 
out-of-order sequences (see also Supplementary Note 1).
T o rescue replay in this network, it is sufficient to simply inhibit 
the distractor neuron (orange) at replay time (Fig. 4c, top). If distrac-
tor neurons are not activated, side paths through the network are 
blocked, and the network dynamics would simply return a walk through 
the structure of the world. How does the network select only those 
neurons corresponding to distractors for inhibition compared to 
neurons encoding part of the structure of the world? We predict that, 
in the online period, the network must represent all schemas because 
it cannot know a priori which schemas will be part of the structure of 
the world and which will turn out to be distractors. Instead, it must 
learn selective suppression of schemas that turn out to be distractors, 
motivating the need for inhibitory plasticity.
T o understand why inhibition accumulates on distractor neurons, 
consider the population firing rate as the mouse navigates the linear 
track. Because place cells tile the track, the average population firing 
rate will be near uniform in the absence of any distractor input. When 
a distractor input is received by the network, the additional excitatory 
currents will directly drive both PC and IN populations (see Supple -
mentary Tables 1–3 for connectivity profiles), causing a local increase 
in the population firing rates for both cell types relative to the baseline 
condition of only structured input. As the distractor input moves from 
lap to lap, the network sees this local increase in firing rate among both 
PC and IN populations at different regions of the track (Fig. 4d). This 
local increase compresses the spike timings of PCs and INs during dis-
tractor input, increasing the rate in change of inhibitory weight onto 
PCs (Fig. 4e). This phenomenon potentiates the weights of inhibitory 
afferents to both place and distractor cells; however, because, at each 
lap, a different subset of place cells will be active during the distractor 
input, the inhibition onto the place cell population is distributed. In 
contrast, total inhibitory weight onto cue cells is increased in every trial 
as the same population of cue cells will be activated (Fig. 4f).
Simulations from our detailed biophysical model support this 
accumulation of inhibition onto cue cells: we found that inhibitory 
weights and IPSCs onto cue cells are significantly higher than onto 
place cells (Fig. 3a–c ). This observation suggests that the circuit 
self-organizes to generate powerful feedforward inhibition onto cue 
cells, which is sufficient to suppress the effect of distractor inputs 
during electrophysiological events, such as ripples.
Experimental verification of model predictions
Finally, we sought to test predictions of our model experimentally. We 
aimed to test our major prediction that representations with unpre-
dictable statistics accumulate inhibition and are suppressed during 
replay as a result. Our strategy was to optogenetically implant a sparse 
population of artificial cue cells into the network in the online phase, 
subsequently examining whether these artificial cue cells become 
suppressed in the offline phase. We carried out experiments with 
direct optogenetic stimulation of sparse subsets of PCs either paired 
with random sensory cue presentation or without cue presentation 
(Fig. 5a–d). In agreement with our model predictions, we found that 
cue presentation paired with PC stimulation was sufficient to induce 
suppression of optogenetically stimulated PCs during SWRs (Fig. 5e,f 
and Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, optogenetic PC stimulation  
unpaired with sensory stimulation was not sufficient to induce SWR  
suppression; instead, similar to our original observation for cue-  
responsive CA3 PCs in T erada et al.57, these cells showed increased SWR- 
related recruitment (Fig. 5e,f and Supplementary Fig. 8). T ogether, 
these results imply that sensory input-driven pre-synaptic inhibition 
and excitatory post-synaptic activation are both necessary to induce 
correlative Hebbian plasticity of inhibitory inputs onto the PCs.
We subsequently asked whether distractor suppression through 
inhibitory plasticity is a general phenomenon that is present outside 
of CA3/CA2, including circuits without strong excitatory recurrent 
connectivity, because our model predicts that the locus of plasticity 
for distractor suppression is at the I  → E synapse. In agreement with 
this prediction, we found that random cue presentation paired with 
optogenetic stimulation of PCs in hippocampal area CA1 also led to sup-
pressed recruitment of paired PCs (Fig. 5a–f) and increased recruitment 
of unpaired PCs during SWRs (Supplementary Fig 8) in CA1, similar to 
our previous findings in CA3/CA2 (ref. 57). These results demonstrate 
that distractor suppression is present in hippocampal circuits with 
different levels of excitatory connectivity.
Lastly, these experiments also probed whether plasticity alone is 
sufficient to produce de novo feature selectivity. Although it is known 
that PCs receive multiple input streams, it may still be the case that 
cells must be developmentally wired to become cue cells. However, 
we found that stable cue responses can be entrained onto arbitrary 
PCs via optogenetic stimulation during cue presentation (Fig. 5d). T o 
our knowledge, this is the first time that non-spatial representations 
have been artificially induced in hippocampal PCs. These results pro-
vide support to the conceptual model that PCs likely receive a rich set 
of place and sensory cue inputs, and feature selectivity is acquired 
through rapid plasticity of a subset of these inputs.
Discussion
The precise content of replay, and its role in learning, remains one 
of the main open questions in the study of the hippocampus. Replay 
has thus far primarily been studied in highly regular and determin -
istic experimental environments. However, repeated exposures to 
the same environment in the real world are not the same: although 
natural environments contain statistical regularities, these regularities 
are obscured in imperfect or incomplete observations. A fundamen-
tal challenge of learning in the real world is abstracting generaliz -
able, low-dimensional representations for long-term storage amid 
a continuous barrage of noisy sensory input 55. Here we show that a 
spike-time-dependent L TP rule at CA3 GABAergic synapses is suffi-
cient to enable consolidation of the statistical structure of the world 
from noisy experience. Using a three-pronged approach of spiking 
network, biophysical and normative modeling, we demonstrate that 
this mechanism is biologically plausible and interpretable and repro-
duces experimental findings. Our model makes the prediction that a 
‘test neuron’ with an artificially induced, statistically unpredictable 
firing field will accumulate inhibition, which we verified using a sparse 
optogenetic induction strategy. Finally, we simplified the intuitive 
notion of ‘statistical learning’ by the hippocampus into a statistical 
inference problem that must be solved using biological primitives, 
and we argue mathematically that the sSTDP-based biological algo-
rithm that we propose correctly solves this inference problem in the 
asymptotic limit of observations.
We think that the model-based replay framework we propose here 
illustrates that the specific experience and predictive/planning repre-
sentation viewpoints of replay are complementary. According to our 
conceptual model, replay samples from the animal’s working model of 
the generalizable structure of its world, synthesizing elements of both 
of these theories to explain how replay can be useful for planning53,54, 
even though representations from distal past experience, which are 
not imminently behaviorally relevant, are sometimes replayed 49–52 .  
Our results resonate with the findings of a high-level normative study by

spatial, auditory, social or more general abstract sequences and rela-

way. Once such representations are organized, the A → B relationships 
between them can be learned incrementally even if gross sequences 
are not repeated. Such a learning rule allows previously unobserved 
sequences to be hypothesized and replayed in the brain. Our model 
makes the prediction that world structures that are fundamentally 
non-spatial, corresponding to arbitrary relational rules obeying a 
well-defined distance metric, can also be learned and replayed by the 
hippocampus via this mechanism.
Work on Hebbian learning rules in the cortex has focused pri -
marily on glutamatergic synapses onto principal cells 36,77. Plasticity 
at the GABAergic synapse has gained greater recognition78, although 
whether the predominant effect is potentiation38,39, depression48,79–84  
or some combination 85 requires clarification in vivo. Compellingly 
for our work here, D’amour and Froemke 86 showed a symmetric 
spike-time-dependent L TP rule for the potentiation of inhibitory syn-
apses in auditory cortex. Our results indicate that the phenomenon of 
replay generalization is robust for a family of inhibitory L TP rules (Sup-
plementary Figs. 2 and 3), providing further support for the biological 
plausibility of this rule.
A limitation of this work is that we treated INs as a uniform popu-
lation, whereas, in reality, distinct subtypes of INs play very differ -
ent computational roles in the hippocampus 60,87– 90. The size of the 
IN population required to achieve this effect in a spiking network of 
thousands of neurons is comparatively small; therefore, it is possible 
that the inhibitory plasticity mechanism that we describe is localized 
to a particular subtype. Our model predicts that the relevant IN popu-
lation will respond to cues during exploration and will be recruited to 
SWRs at rest. Dissecting the subtypes shaped by this inhibitory L TP rule 
represents an important avenue for future experimental work. A pre-
requisite of our model is that a subset of GABAergic INs in CA3 exhibits 
activation by sensory stimulation but stable SWR recruitment (as the 
locus of plasticity is at the GABAergic synapse). Indeed, in Vancura 
et al.90, we recently identified the cholecystokinin-expressing INs as one 
such candidate population (see Extended Data Fig. 9 in Vancura et al.90).
Our models are inspired by the recurrent CA3 circuitry, but the 
mechanisms described here are likely to extend to the recurrent cir -
cuitry of area CA2 as well, where SWRs can be initiated26. Furthermore, 
our experimental results demonstrate that distractor suppression is 
also present in area CA1, which contains far fewer recurrent collater-
als compared to CA3/CA2, and where most replay studies have been 
performed to date30,53,91. These results suggest that distractor suppres-
sion through inhibitory plasticity generalizes to other hippocampal 
subregions. Future experiments may explore whether this represents 
a general coding principle that extends beyond the hippocampus.
Our experiments focused on awake replay91, but our model may 
be extensible to replay occurring during sleep53,92. Several differences 
have been highlighted between awake and asleep replay: awake replays 
occur in both the forward and backward directions and more faith -
fully recapitulate experience, whereas sleep replays are biased to the 
forward direction and are noisier92. These differences may be driven 
by differences in input between arousal states: in the awake animal, 
spatially localized sensory input has been suggested as a major con -
tributor to localized awake replay—in particular, reverse replay starting 
from the animal’s current location14,15,93 or once the animal arrives at a 
reward50,51,94– 96—whereas hippocampal inputs may be more stochastic 
during sleep. It has been proposed that these differences make awake 
replay more suited to planning and sleep replay to memory consoli -
dation92. For simplicity, we simulate replay with random input in the 
present study, but injecting location-specific offline input biases replay 
starts to that location, consistent with experimental observations14,15,93, 
suggesting that simple modifications of our model may lend them -
selves easily to studies of replay in these distinct states.
Abnormalities in hippocampal neural reactivation, SWRs and 
inhibitory transmission have been demonstrated in a variety of 
neuropsychiatric disorders, including temporal lobe epilepsy, schizo-
phrenia and autism97–105 . In the present work, we identified a potential 
link between abnormalities of inhibitory synaptic transmission and 
memory deficits both in general. As our mechanism links the plasticity 
of inhibitory synapses to the high-level learning of world structure, any 
disruption to inhibitory plasticity may contribute to the cognitive defi-
cits observed in this disorder and others106,107. Electroencephalography 
(EEG) signatures of epilepsy have also been associated with the engage-
ment of highly coupled pathological microcircuits 108– 110; a potential  
‘pathological transformation’ is hypothesized to occur from physio-
logical sharp-wave ripples to interictal epileptiform discharges 111.  
We speculate that these pathological coupled ensemble events may 
be the result of a failure of inhibition onto irrelevant representations 
during replay.
We provide a mechanistic narrative of how the hippocampus may 
incrementally assemble a generalized representation of an environ -
ment despite only ever receiving noisy observations. The concurrent 
demands of expressiveness and flexibility provide insight into why 
a plasticity mechanism, in particular one of inhibitory synapses, is 
necessary for robust learning in the face of distractors—to wit, the 
brain cannot know a priori which stimuli are important until it has 
sufficiently sampled the world. The same stimulus that is a distractor 
in one context may be essential to understanding the structure of the 
world in the next. Therefore, the hippocampus must be able to robustly 
represent all stimuli during online learning as they are received and 
then selectively consolidate only those representations that model the 
conserved underlying structure of the ensemble of its experience. We 
make several novel, experimentally approachable predictions using 
this framework that will hopefully provide insight into the low-level 
mechanisms underlying high-level cognitive functions, such as mem-
ory consolidation and generalization. We think that the framework we 
present here provides a bridge between the synaptic and the cognitive 
levels of our understanding of learning and, if experimentally validated, 
could shed light on the fundamental question of how distributed, local 
learning rules can structure initially random neuronal networks to 
perform highly complex cognitive tasks.

Methods
Experimental methods
All experiments were conducted in accordance with National Insti-
tutes of Health (NIH) guidelines and with the approval of the Columbia 
University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Imaging 
experiments were performed on 2–4-month-old, heterozygous adult 
male and female wild-type mice (C57BL/6J) from The Jackson Labora-
tory (strain no. 000664). Mice were housed on a 12-h light/dark cycle 
in groups of 2–5 mice (temperature, 22–23 ∘C; humidity, 40%) and indi-
vidually after surgery for implantation. Experiments were performed 
during the dark portion of the cycle.
Viruses
We used GCaMP8m (pGP-AAV-syn-jGCaMP8m-WPRE, https://www.
addgene.org/162375/), Cre-dependent ChRmine (pAAV-Ef1α -DIO- 
ChRmine-mScarlet-WPRE, https://www.addgene.org/130998/) and 
Cre under hSyn promoter (pENN.AAV.hSyn.Cre.WPRE.hGH, https://
www.addgene.org/105553/). For sparse excitatory opsin labeling, we 
diluted Cre virus with sterilized saline (1:2,000) and co-injected the 
virus mixtures containing equal amounts of the three individual rAAV 
constructs above.
Surgical procedure
Viral delivery to hippocampal area CA1 and CA2/3 and implantation 
of headposts and imaging cannula were described previously 57,75,90. 
In brief, 2–4-month-old mice were anesthetized under isofluorane 
and placed into a stereotaxic apparatus. Meloxicam and bupivacaine 
were administered subcutaneously to minimize discomfort. Using a 
Nanoject syringe, viruses were delivered by stereotactically injecting 
50 nl (10-nl pulses) of rAAV at multiple injection sites in left dorsal CA1 
(−2.1 mm AP; −1.5 mm ML; −0.9 mm, −1.05 mm and −1.2 mm DV relative 
to bregma) or CA2/3 (−1.35 mm AP, −1.6 mm ML, −2.1 mm, −1.9 mm DV 
and −1.6 mm AP, −1.9 mm ML, −2.2 mm, −2.0 mm DV relative to bregma). 
The mice were allowed to recover in their home cage for 3 d before the 
window/headpost implant. They were then surgically implanted with 
a custom metal headpost along with an imaging cannula (diameter, 
3.0 mm; height, 1.5 mm for CA1 and 2.0 mm for CA2/3) over the injec-
tion locations. In brief, mice were anesthetized under isofluorane and 
placed into a stereotaxic apparatus. The skull was exposed after subcu-
taneous administration of meloxicam and bupivacaine. A 3-mm crani-
otomy was done over the left dorsal hippocampus, centered between 
the injection coordinates. Dura and cortical layers were gently removed 
under visual guidance while flushing with ice-cold cortex buffer. The 
imaging cannula was inserted through the surgical opening in the 
skull. Finally, the metal headpost was affixed to the skull with dental 
cement. The mice received a 1.0-ml subcutaneous injection of saline 
and recovered in their home cage while heat was applied. The mice were 
monitored for 3 d after the operation until behavioral training began.
Histology and confocal imaging
After the completion of imaging experiments, mice were transcar-
dially perfused with 20 ml of ice-cold PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific),  
followed by 20 ml of ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Micro-
scopy Sciences). Brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde over-
night, washed with PBS three times for 10 min each and coronally 
sectioned at a thickness of 100 μm using a vibratome (Leica, VT1200S). 
Sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in 
PBS for 60 min and incubated with a rabbit primary antibody against 
PCP4 (1:500, HPA005792, Sigma-Aldrich) in the blocking solution for 16 h 
at 4 ∘C. Sections were washed three times for 10 min with PBS and incu-
bated with Alexa Fluor 647 donkey secondary antibody against rabbit  
IgG (1:500, 711-605-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch) in the blocking 
solution for 6 h at room temperature. After being washed three times 
for 10 min each in PBS, the sections were incubated with 1 μg ml−1  DAPI 
in PBS including 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. 
The sections were then washed three times for 5 min each in PBS at 
room temperature. Finally, sections were mounted on glass slides in 
Fluoromount-G aqueous mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) 
and coverslipped. A Nikon A1 confocal microscope was used to acquire 
multichannel fluorescence images of the immunolabeled tissue sec-
tions; 405-nm, 488-nm, 561-nm and 640-nm laser lines were used for 
excitation. Each channel was acquired sequentially with a ×10/0.45-NA 
Plan Apo objective (Nikon) at ×1.0 zoom; 2,048 × 2,048 pixel images 
were acquired every 3 μm through the entire depth of the tissue sec-
tions. The resulting four-channel z-stacks were viewed in ImageJ (NIH).
Behavioral paradigm with closed-loop optogenetic 
stimulation
Mice were head fixed above a low-friction, lightweight running  
wheel76 in a behavioral context constructed similarly to the random cue 
without tactile cue condition (RC-NS) in T erada et al.57. An odor cue was 
presented pseudo-randomly for 10 trials. Each presentation lasted 1 s, 
and presentations were separated by a pseudo-random inter-stimulus 
interval of 40–60 s. The odorant R-(−)carvone (Sigma-Aldrich) dis -
solved in mineral oil was passed through tubing in front of the mouse’s 
snout through a mineral oil-infused stream of compressed air that was 
evacuated by vacuum at the same speed it was delivered to avoid pro-
longed exposure. For the first five trials, optogenetic photostimulation 
was triggered by onset of odor presentation and sustained until odor 
removal. For the next five trials, odor presentation was conducted 
without photostimulation to test the induced cue response of the 
stimulated cells. In the control session, photostimulation alone was 
delivered at random 40–60-s intervals for five trials. The pre and post 
15-min imaging sessions were conducted to monitor SWR-related 
dynamics of the stimulated cells before and after the stimulation ses-
sions. Animal locomotion behavior (active running versus passive rest) 
was monitored via velocity.
Identification of optogenetic-evoked response, induced cue 
selectivity and SWRs
Stimulated cell detection.  A trial-averaged fluorescence peri-  
stimulation time histogram (PSTH) was computed for each cell for 
odor presentations paired with photostimulation and without photo-
stimulation. The principal components were extracted from the PSTHs 
of all imaged cells. We confirmed each first principal component  
represented cell activation in response to cue presentation (whether 
optogenetically evoked or induced selectivity). We carried out k-means 
clustering in the space of P C1stim versus P C1unstim to classify the PCs 
based on ‘stimulated versus unstimulated’ and ‘odor responding versus 
non-responding’ factors (Supplementary Fig. 8b).
Detection and comparison of SWR-associated dynamics.  We 
smoothed and averaged (five-bin window, Gaussian filter) the ΔF /F0 
values of unstimulated cells to identify candidate highly synchro -
nous events (HSEs) associated with SWRs in CA1 and CA2/3. Frames 
associated with locomotion were removed, and HSEs were defined as 
transients on the population average that exceeded the mean by at least 
3 s.d. Then, peri-SWR-HSE PSTHs of the stimulated cells were calculated 
from the pre-rest and post-rest sessions, concatenated to extract princi
-
pal components in each condition (Cue-STIM and Random) and subarea 
(CA1 and CA2/3). We confirmed that the first principal components 
of all groups represented SWR-associated transients as previously 
reported57. Finally, we compared these SWR component coefficients 
between pre-rest and post-rest sessions within the stimulated cells.

generating spontaneous replay, similar to previous work32 (see refer-

were run in Brian 2 (ref. 58).

Biophysical model
The biophysical model of hippocampal CA3 was built from 260 CA3 
PCs and 30 fast-spiking perisomatic-targeting INs. Each neuron was 
represented as a multi-compartment model, where each compartment 
mapped to various physical locations, including soma, basal dendrites 
and apical dendrites. Additionally, each neuron contained biophysical 
ion channel models with realistic conductances, thereby allowing the  
user to track the continuous intracellular voltage trace within each 
compartment. PCs and INs received external input from multiple  
afferent sources modeled as Poisson spike generators, including the 
MEC, the LEC and the dentate gyrus (DG) via MFs.
The neurons were placed into a virtual CA3 volume divided into 
stratum lacunosum moleculare (SLM), statrum radiatum (SR), stratum 
lucidum (SL), stratum pyramidale (SP) and stratum oriens (SO). The 
soma position of PCs and INs as well as specific connectivity patterns 
among PCs, INs and external inputs (convergence and specific site of 
contact) were allocated according to known experimental data. For fur-
ther details on biophysical parameters, see Supplementary Tables 1–3.
Training and replay. Similar to the spiking network model, a virtual 
circular track was generated and simulations were performed with the 
mouse moving at a constant velocity. All simulations were run in the 
NEURON environment113 with dt = 0.1 ms.
For training, 80% of CA3 PCs were selected as place cells and 
received pre-allocated and spatially tuned input from external MEC 
and MF sources. Specifically, MFs carried place inputs, whereas MEC 
afferents carried grid inputs. The diameter of place fields was selected 
to cover 10% of the track, tuned such that the mean firing rate at the 
center of the place field was 20 Hz (see spiking network model for 
equations). Grid inputs were instantiated with similar parameters 
but were additionally allowed to cover the virtual track in a periodic 
manner with a grid spacing equal to 10% of the track. The remaining 
20% of CA3 PCs were selected as cue cells and received pre-allocated 
distractor input from external LEC. The distractor input was activated 
at a random location once per lap.
During the offline epoch, we removed spatially structured and dis-
tractor input from all external sources. Instead, CA3 neurons received 
random Poisson spiking input, and spontaneous replay was observed.

Quantitative analysis
Offline cue–place aggregate analysis.  The full offline epoch was 
divided into non-overlapping bins equating to 100 ms of simulation 
time. The number of spikes for cue cells and place cells in any given 
bin was extracted and plotted on a 2D histogram. Pearson’s correlation 
coefficients and P values were computed, and a two-tailed t -test was 
used to determine if the cue and place cell subnetworks were function-
ally connected (P < 0.05) or not (P > 0.05).
Peri-event time histograms were computed according to the 
z-scores of the bin spike counts computed for the 2D histograms. 
Time bins with a place cell z-score of 1 or greater were considered to 
be occurrences of HSEs. Pearson’s correlation coefficients and P values 
were computed to test for correlation between z-scores of place cell and 
cue cell activity over time periods starting at 2 s before and ending 2 s 
after the occurrence of each HSE. Cue cell suppression was quantified 
as negative correlation with P < 0.05.

Statistics and reproducibility
Statistical details of comparisons are specified in either the main text or 
the figure legends. No statistical methods were used to predetermine 
sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previ-
ous publications57. Mice showing poor imaging windows (low-quality 
viral expression or scarring), animals that did not display clear SWRs in 
their local field potential (LFP) signals and those that did not progress 
along the behavioral paradigms described above were excluded before 
commencing experiments. The investigators were not blinded to allo-
cation during experiments and outcome assessment. Unless otherwise 
noted, box plots represent median and interquartile range, and whisk-
ers extend to cover the distribution without outliers (defined as points 
above 1.5× the interquartile range below or above the box edges). For 
comparisons between two populations, a paired sample or unpaired 
t-test was applied if the data points followed a normal distribution. In 
the instances where parametric tests were used, distributions were 
assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. T o analyze 
data that were not normally distributed, a Mann–Whitney U-test was 
used. All tests were two-sided unless otherwise indicated in the figure 
legends; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001. Data analysis was performed 
and figures were generated using custom-made software in Python 3.8.
